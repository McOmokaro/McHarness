# Pothole vs Well

Two holes in the ground. Both are holes. Only one has water.

- A **pothole** is **wide and shallow**. Everyone drives over it; nobody stops. Broad appeal, no depth — you can't charge much because there's nothing down there to pull up.
- A **well** is **narrow and deep**. Fewer people walk to it, but it reaches water. Narrow audience, real depth — this is where high-ticket offers live.

The mistake almost everyone makes: chasing the pothole because it's bigger. Breadth feels like market size. It's usually just the absence of a niche.

## How to tell which one you're standing in

Score the topic on three axes. A well scores high on **Depth**; a pothole scores high on **Breadth** and low on Depth.

| Axis | Pothole (bad) | Well (good) |
|---|---|---|
| **Breadth** — how many people care at all | huge, "everyone" | small but specific |
| **Depth** — how much is underneath | thin; one idea, fully explained in a tweet | deep; months of material, hard-won, layered |
| **Consequence** — what's at stake if the buyer gets it wrong | trivial (a preference) | expensive (money, health, reputation, time) |

Ask:
1. **Can I write 50 pieces or teach 10 hours on this without repeating myself?** No → pothole.
2. **Does getting it wrong cost the buyer something real?** No → pothole.
3. **Is there a specific person I can name, not "small-business owners" but "single-location dental practices losing $8k/mo to no-shows"?** No → pothole.

## The failure patterns

- **The "everyone" trap:** "personal finance", "AI", "marketing", "fitness". Infinite breadth, zero depth — you're competing with the whole internet and can only charge commodity prices.
- **The category-as-topic:** naming a field instead of a problem. "Bookkeeping" is a pothole; "stopping the cash leak in clinics that bill insurance late" is a well.
- **The one-idea topic:** if you said everything there is to say in a paragraph, there's no well — you have a tip, not an offer.

## Turning a pothole into a well: drill, don't widen

You do not fix a pothole by making it bigger. You drill.

1. **Pick a sub-audience** — the narrower, the deeper. (Not "realtors" → "realtors who list luxury properties in cash-heavy markets".)
2. **Pick a sub-problem with consequence** — one that costs money/health and worsens over time (pair with the **pain-extractor** skill).
3. **Go down until you hit water** — the point where you know things 95% of practitioners don't, from doing the work, and can charge accordingly.
4. **Keep it narrow.** A well you can serve for years beats a pothole everyone drives past.

## The test, in one line

> **If you can't name the person and the price their mistake costs them, it's a pothole. Drill until you can.**

Wells are uncomfortable at first because they look small. That smallness *is* the moat — it's why you can charge ten times what the pothole-sellers charge, and why you can't be replaced by the next generalist.
